\lecture{4}{2026-09-16}{Euler--Maclaurin summation and complex analysis}

In the previous lecture, we began exploring asymptotic approximations:
when a sequence has an explicit closed form, Taylor expansions often provide asymptotic terms;
when a sequence is defined by a sum of simple terms, integral approximations can do the same.
We first conclude our discussion of real-variable summation techniques by refining integral approximations via the Euler--Maclaurin summation formula, and then introduce the fundamentals of complex analysis to extract asymptotics directly from generating functions.

\begin{theorem}[Integral comparison for monotone functions, {\cite[Chapter 11]{MelczerNotes}}]
    Let \(a < b\) be integers, and let \(f\) be continuous on \([a-1, b+1]\).
    If \(f\) is increasing on \([a-1, b+1]\), then
    \[
        \int_{a-1}^{b} f(x) \, dx \le \sum_{k=a}^{b} f(k) \le \int_{a}^{b+1} f(x) \, dx.
    \]
    If \(f\) is decreasing on \([a-1, b+1]\), then
    \[
        \int_{a}^{b+1} f(x) \, dx \le \sum_{k=a}^{b} f(k) \le \int_{a-1}^{b} f(x) \, dx.
    \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[{\cite[Chapter 11]{MelczerNotes}}]
    If \(f\) is monotone on \([a-1, b+1]\) and \(|f(x)| \le M\) on \([a-1, b+1]\), then
    \[
        \left| \sum_{k=a}^{b} f(k) - \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \right| \le M.
    \]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    If \(f\) is increasing, subtracting \(\int_a^b f(x) \, dx\) from each part of the inequality in the theorem yields
    \[
        -M \le \int_{a-1}^{a} f(x) \, dx \le \sum_{k=a}^{b} f(k) - \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \le \int_{b}^{b+1} f(x) \, dx \le M.
    \]
    If \(f\) is decreasing, the bounds reverse, leading to the same absolute bound since \(|f(x)| \le M\).
\end{proof}

\section*{Euler--Maclaurin summation formula}

The Bernoulli numbers \(B_k\) are defined by the exponential generating function
\[
    \frac{t}{e^t - 1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} B_k \frac{t^k}{k!} = 1 - \frac{1}{2} t + \frac{1}{12} t^2 - \frac{1}{720} t^4 + \dots
\]
In particular, \(B_0 = 1\), \(B_1 = -1/2\), \(B_2 = 1/6\), and \(B_k = 0\) for all odd \(k \ge 3\).

\begin{theorem}[Euler--Maclaurin summation formula]
Let \(a < b\) be integers, and suppose \(f\) is \(m\)-times continuously differentiable on \([a, b]\).
Then
\[
    \sum_{k=a}^{b} f(k)
    = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx 
    + \frac{f(a) + f(b)}{2}
    + \sum_{k=2}^m \frac{B_k}{k!} \left( f^{(k-1)}(b) - f^{(k-1)}(a) \right)
    + R_m,
\]
where the remainder term \(R_m\) satisfies
\[
    |R_m| \le \frac{2 \zeta(m)}{(2\pi)^m} \int_{a}^{b} |f^{(m)}(x)| \, dx,
\]
and \(\zeta(m) = \sum_{n \ge 1} n^{-m}\) is the Riemann zeta function.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
    The boundary term \(\frac{f(a) + f(b)}{2}\) corresponds to the trapezoidal rule approximation.
    Furthermore, because all odd Bernoulli numbers vanish for \(k \ge 3\) (i.e., \(B_3 = B_5 = B_7 = \dots = 0\)), all odd-indexed terms in the summation vanish.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    Let \(f(x) = x^2\), and consider \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} k^2\). We previously showed by integral comparison that \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n^3}{3} + O(n^2)\).
    Applying the Euler--Maclaurin formula with \(m=3\) on \([0, n]\):
    \[
        \sum_{k=0}^{n} k^2 = \int_0^n x^2 \, dx + \frac{f(0) + f(n)}{2} + \frac{B_2}{2!} (f'(n) - f'(0)) + \frac{B_3}{3!} (f''(n) - f''(0)) + R_3.
    \]
    Here \(\int_0^n x^2 \, dx = \frac{n^3}{3}\), \(\frac{f(0) + f(n)}{2} = \frac{n^2}{2}\), and with \(f'(x) = 2x\):
    \[
        \frac{B_2}{2!} (f'(n) - f'(0)) = \frac{1/6}{2} (2n - 0) = \frac{n}{6}.
    \]
    Furthermore, \(B_3 = 0\), and since \(f^{(3)}(x) = 0\), the remainder term vanishes:
    \[
        |R_3| \le \frac{2 \zeta(3)}{(2\pi)^3} \int_{0}^{n} |f^{(3)}(x)| \, dx = 0.
    \]
    Therefore, Euler--Maclaurin reproduces the exact formula:
    \[
        \sum_{k=0}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n^3}{3} + \frac{n^2}{2} + \frac{n}{6} = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}.
    \]
\end{example}

While these real-variable summation techniques are effective for sums of explicit algebraic terms, they do not easily generalize to implicitly defined sequences or more complex combinatorial structures.
The rest of this course will focus on methods where these classical techniques do not apply, extracting asymptotics directly from generating functions.

\section*{Analytic Combinatorics}

Starting here, we follow the approach of \cite{Melczer2021} and extract asymptotics directly from generating functions, rather than relying on explicit closed forms or real summation formulas.
To do so, we study generating functions as analytic functions of a complex variable.

Recall that a complex number \(z \in \mathbb{C}\) can be written in Cartesian coordinates as \(z = x + iy\) (with \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\)) or in polar coordinates as \(z = r e^{i\theta}\), where \(r = |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) is the modulus and \(\theta = \arg(z)\) is the argument, defined up to integer multiples of \(2\pi\).

Working in the complex plane \(\mathbb{C}\) is essential to understand the domain of convergence and singular behavior of power series, as demonstrated by the following example.

\begin{example}[Why complex analysis is necessary]
    The geometric series
    \[
        \frac{1}{1-z} = \sum_{n \ge 0} z^n
    \]
    converges for \(|z| < 1\), with a pole at \(z = 1\), which limits the radius of convergence to \(1\).
    
    Now consider the function \(f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2}\) on the real line. The function \(f\) is infinitely differentiable everywhere on \(\mathbb{R}\), yet its Maclaurin series
    \[
        \frac{1}{1+x^2} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (-1)^n x^{2n}
    \]
    converges only for \(|x| < 1\). From the real perspective, this finite radius of convergence is mysterious since the function has no singularities on \(\mathbb{R}\).
    In the complex plane, however, \(f(z) = \frac{1}{1+z^2}\) has poles at \(z = \pm i\), which lie at distance \(1\) from the origin. The presence of complex singularities explains the limitation on the radius of convergence.
\end{example}

Our presentation of complex analysis is centered around power series.

\begin{definition}[Open disk, {\cite[Appendix, p.~76]{Melczer2021}}]
An \emph{open disk} centered at \(a \in \mathbb{C}\) of radius \(r > 0\) is the set
\[
    D_a^{(r)} = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z - a| < r \}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Open set, domain, and neighborhood, {\cite[Appendix, p.~76]{Melczer2021}}]
A set \(O \subseteq \mathbb{C}\) is \emph{open} if for every \(a \in O\), there exists \(r > 0\) such that \(D_a^{(r)} \subseteq O\).
A connected open set is called a \emph{domain}.
A \emph{neighborhood} of \(a \in \mathbb{C}\) is an open set containing \(a\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Analytic function, {\cite[Definition 2.3]{Melczer2021}}]
A function \(f(z)\) is \emph{analytic} at \(z = a\) if there exists a neighborhood \(N\) of \(a\) such that \(f(z)\) is representable by a convergent power series
\[
    f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (z - a)^n
\]
for all \(z \in N\).
A function \(f(z)\) is \emph{analytic} on a domain \(\Omega\) if it is analytic at every point of \(\Omega\).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    The function \(f(z) = \frac{1}{1-z}\) is represented by \(f(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} z^n\) for \(|z| < 1\), so it is analytic on the open unit disk \(D_0^{(1)}\).

    Expanding instead around \(a = -1\):
    \[
        f(z) = \frac{1}{2 - (z + 1)} = \frac{1/2}{1 - \frac{z + 1}{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{(z + 1)^n}{2^n},
    \]
    which converges for \(|z + 1| < 2\). Thus, \(f\) is also analytic on the disk \(D_{-1}^{(2)}\).
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Complex differentiability, {\cite[Appendix, p.~76]{Melczer2021}}]
    A function \(f\) is analytic on a domain \(\Omega\) if and only if it is complex-differentiable on \(\Omega\); that is, the derivative
    \[
        f'(a) = \lim_{z \to a} \frac{f(z) - f(a)}{z - a}
    \]
    exists for all \(a \in \Omega\).
    The complex derivative satisfies the standard sum, product, quotient, and chain rules.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Cauchy--Riemann equations, {\cite[Appendix, p.~76]{Melczer2021}}]
    Let \(f(x + iy) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y)\) for real-valued functions \(u\) and \(v\). If \(f\) is analytic on a domain \(\Omega\), then \(u\) and \(v\) satisfy the Cauchy--Riemann equations
    \[
        \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}.
    \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Identity theorem, {\cite[Lemma 3.2]{Melczer2021}}]
    If \(f\) and \(g\) are analytic functions on a domain \(\Omega\) and agree on a non-empty open subset of \(\Omega\), then \(f = g\) on all of \(\Omega\).
\end{proposition}
