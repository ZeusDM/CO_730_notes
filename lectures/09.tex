For a rational function, it's ``easy'' to find asymptotics if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item unique root closest to the origin, or
    \item among the roots closest to the origin, there is a unique root of maximal multiplicity.
\end{itemize}

How do I find the roots closest to the origin? What is the complexity of finding asymptoptics?

\begin{theorem}[Vivandi--Pringsheim Theorem]
    If \(F(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n z^n\) where \(f_n \geq 0\) for all \(n\), then one of its singularities of minimal modulus is a positive real number.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    If \(f_n \geq 0\), then
    \[
    |F(z)| = \left| \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n z^n \right| \leq \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n |z|^n = F(|z|).
    \]

    Thus, if \(\omega\) is a pole singularity of \(F(z)\) of minimal modulus, then if \(z_n \to \omega\) inside the domain of convergence, we have \(|F(z_n)| \to \infty\).
    But then \(|F(|z_n|)| \to \infty\) as well, so \(|z_n| \to |\omega|\).
    Thus, \(|\omega|\) is a singularity of minimal modulus, and \(|\omega|\) is a positive real number.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[\(\mathbb{N}\)-rational sequence]
    The set of \(\mathbb{N}\)-rational sequences is the set of sequences:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item containing \(1\) and \(z\),
        \item closed under addition and multiplication,
        \item closed under the operation \(f \maps \frac{1}{1 - zf}\).
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

These are the generating functions of regular languages.

\begin{theorem}[Berstel]
    If \(F(z)\) is \(\mathbb{N}\)-rational, then its dominant singularities consist of some \(\rho\) and (potentially) some \(\rho \zeta_1, \ldots, \rho \zeta_s\) where \(\zeta_i\) are roots of unity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
    Let \(\theta = \arccos(1/3)\).
    Then,
    \begin{align*}
        F(z) &= \frac{2(1+3z)^2}{(1-9z)(1+14z+81z^2)} \\
        &= \frac{1/2}{1-9z^{2i\theta}} + \frac{1/2}{1-9z^{-2i\theta}} - \frac{1}{1-9z},
    \end{align*}
    which is not \(\mathbb{N}\)-rational because \(e^{2i\theta}\) is not a root of unity.
    Nevertheless, its coefficients are nonnegative integers.
\end{example}

\begin{corollary}
    If \(F\) is \(\mathbb{N}\)-rational, then there exists \(p > 0\)
    such that
    \[
        F(z) = S_0(z^p) + z S_1(z^p) + \cdots + z^{p-1} S_{p-1}(z^p),
    \]
    where \(S_0, \ldots, S_{p-1}\) are \(\mathbb{N}\)-rational and have a unique dominant singularity.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[``Meta Theorem'']
    Any ``naturally occurring'' rational combinatorial generating function is \(\mathbb{N}\)-rational.
\end{theorem}

How fast can we find asymptotics?

The most expensive step is finding the roots of minimal modulus.

Let \(P(z) \in \mathbb{Z}[z]\) be a polynomial of degree \(d\) with coefficients of size at most \(2^h\).

\begin{lemma}
    If \(P(\alpha) = P(\beta) = 0\), and \(\alpha \neq \beta\), then \(|\alpha - \beta| \geq D_d 2^{-h(d-1)} \), where \(D_d\) is a polynomial in \(d\) of degree \(O(d)\).
\end{lemma}

Therefore, we need to the order of \(hd\) bits of precision to find the roots of \(P\) to sufficient accuracy to distinguish them.

For our asymptotics, we need to separate the moduli of the roots, i.e., 
\[
    \big| |\alpha| - |\beta| \big| \geq \text{something}.
\]
This is still pretty open.
